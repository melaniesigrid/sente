# Design: Coaching — shape commentary, the game archive, and Neo-Human pair go

## Problem Statement

Joseki's house players already talk, but they talk blind. Persona chat in
`src/content/personas.js` is six hand-written line pools (`greet`, `botCapture`,
`userCapture`, `reply`, `win`, `loss`) picked at random in `src/views/Game.jsx`. The bot
never looks at the board. It cannot say why a move was good, cannot name a shape, and
cannot remember anything, because nothing about a finished game is kept.

The ask is three features stacked on one bet:

1. **Live shape commentary.** The bots comment on shapes the player is forming now, in a
   playful, philosophical, simple voice.
2. **A game archive.** Every finished game stored, up to a cap, so the bots can also
   comment on shapes formed in past games, and so a real database has a substrate.
3. **Neo-Human pair go.** Two humans of about 10 kyu pick a strong bot persona (say 7d)
   that alternates turns with them. A new format nobody else offers.

The bet underneath: that coaching is what makes a player choose Joseki.

## Demand Evidence

Claimed as observed behaviour — go players already pay for review, run KaTrain, or ask
stronger players. No specific person was named, so this is category-level evidence, not
verified demand.

The one piece of real, first-party demand evidence in the session is different and better:

> "It started because I belong to the Go Guatemala association. I wanted to create my own
> server to play my friends because the options online are ugly, feel not safe or hard to
> use. Chat and having my friends will be very important and they can be the first users
> soon."

That is a named group of real people, already playing go together, already dissatisfied
with their options. It is the strongest asset in the project. It is also demand for a
**place to play**, not for a coach.

## Status Quo

Answered "all the above": the audience spans hobbyists to professionals, so every
workaround is in play at once — no review at all, AI Sensei and OGS post-game review,
asking a stronger player, and KaTrain on a second monitor.

What none of those do: talk to you **during** the game, in a voice, about **shape** rather
than about score. Every serious go tool deliberately hides analysis during play. That gap
is where this feature lives. It is also the reason the gap exists, and the risk the
experiment below is designed to measure.

## Target User & Narrowest Wedge

**Target user:** a member of the Asociación de Go de Guatemala. Plays club members, wants
a place that is not ugly, not sketchy, and not hard to use, with chat.

**Wedge:** preceded by an experiment, on the founder's instruction:

> "I want to test experimentally just first start adding the extra comments to the chat so
> I can see. Then, start building the chat features etc"

Ship shape commentary into the existing bot chat first, cheaply, play ten games, and find
out whether in-game shape talk is delightful or annoying. That is the fastest available
demand test. The club and chat work follows.

## Constraints

From `CLAUDE.md`, all binding:

- Engine code is pure and framework-free. No React in engine modules. A shape detector is
  engine code.
- Rules never live in a view. If a view needs a rule, the engine gets it first.
- `src/engine/index.js` is the only import surface for views.
- Tests sit beside each engine module. Content is data, verified by `npm test`.
- House players are labeled honestly as bots. Coaching must not blur that.
- No exclamation marks in lesson text. §Voice contract extends that rule to commentary by
  choice, which is a decision, not an inheritance — see the note there.
- Run vitest from PowerShell, not Git Bash.
- Source is CRLF. Scripted multi-line edits must normalise or they silently no-op.

## Premises

1. **The wedge is the club, not the coach** — with an experimental prologue. Order:
   shape-commentary experiment, then club and chat, then the archive, then Neo-Human.
2. **The archive is the foundation under the past-games ask**, and under any future
   database, Neo-Human review, or coaching history. It is ordered after club and chat
   because the club is what produces games worth archiving; archiving solo bot games first
   would build the substrate before the content exists.
3. **Shape commentary is engine-side and deterministic, not LLM-generated.** A detector
   that names real shapes is pure, testable, offline, free, and obeys the engine contract.
   The persona is the voice layer on top: same detected fact, different phrasing per bot.
   An LLM would be non-deterministic, cost money per move, and break the engine's
   server-portability.
4. **A game with the coach talking is a teaching game, and teaching games are unrated.**
   Every persona game today is rated: `conclude` runs `rateAgainst` and writes the profile
   (`src/views/Game.jsx:201-232`), and the result card says "Rated against a house player"
   (`:512`). A bot naming your bad shape mid-game while that game moves your Glicko-2
   rating is assistance, and Joseki's whole positioning is honest ratings. So coaching is a
   toggle, the toggle is visible on the table, and turning it on makes the game unrated and
   says so. Daily duels are excluded entirely — they are a shared, comparable result.
5. **Neo-Human is a seat-model change in the multiplayer Worker, not a coaching feature.**
   Two humans plus a bot sharing one colour, turn rotation, and a Durable Object that
   understands a seat can be occupied by software. It belongs after the club is on the
   server. Neo-Human games are unrated for the same reason as premise 4, and more so.

## Approaches Considered

### Approach A: Inline spike (rejected)

Detect shapes inside `Game.jsx` and push lines into the chat. Rejected: puts a rule in a
view against the repo's first convention, and nothing about it is testable — a coach that
is wrong about a shape is worse than silence.

### Approach B: Engine detector plus voiced phrase library (chosen)

`src/engine/shape.js` exposes a pure detector over the board and the last move, returning
named findings. `src/content/commentary.js` maps findings to lines keyed by shape id and
persona, and holds a pure selector for pacing. One integration point per move in
`Game.jsx`.

### Approach C: Policy-divergence coach (deferred)

Use the already-loaded KataGo human-style net to flag moves far from what a player of that
rank usually plays. Deferred: it comments on choice, not form, so it cannot be
philosophical about shape; it doubles inference per move; it is non-deterministic and so
cannot be tested or replayed. Worth revisiting as a second commentary source once the
detector works.

## Recommended Approach

**Approach B.** It is the only one whose output survives the experiment. The same detector
that speaks during a live game later reads the archive and says "you have played the empty
triangle in four of your last ten games" — the past-games ask, unchanged.

### Detection mechanism

Not a `chainAt` sweep. `chainAt` (`board.js:58`) floods one connected chain of one colour;
most shapes here are local patterns and one of them (ponnuki) is four separate chains that
merely touch a point. The detector is:

- a **local neighbourhood match** around the move just played, O(1) per move, for the
  pattern shapes. `empty-triangle` reads the four 2×2 windows containing the move;
  `tigers-mouth` reads each empty point adjacent to the move and counts that point's own
  orthogonal neighbours, which spans a 2×3 window; plus
- **`chainAt` only for the mouth's guards.** `dumpling` turned out not to need it: the four
  stones of a solid 2x2 are necessarily one chain, so the block test alone is enough.
  `tigers-mouth` does need it, for the reason in the notes below - at most three chain
  floods, and only once a candidate mouth has already been found.

### Module contract

`src/engine/shape.js`, pure, no I/O, no React:

```js
/**
 * @param {Board} board      position AFTER the move
 * @param {{c:number,r:number}|null} move  the stone just played; null for pass/resign/setup
 * @param {{ color: "b"|"w", captured?: [number,number][] }} opts
 *        color    — the colour that just played
 *        captured — stones removed by this move (rec.lastCaptured), for capture-shaped patterns
 * @returns {Finding[]}
 */
detectShapes(board, move, opts)

// Finding: {
//   id: string,                    // shape id, e.g. "empty-triangle"
//   color: "b"|"w",                // owner of `stones`
//   stones: [number, number][],    // the stones that make the shape
//   severity: "praise"|"note"|"warn",
// }
```

`move === null` returns `[]`. No moves yet returns `[]`. The scoring phase is never
queried.

### First shape set — three, not eight

The experiment asks one binary question. Three shapes answer it as well as eight, with a
third of the false-positive surface.

| id | severity | detection | why it earns a line |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `empty-triangle` | `note` | one of the four 2×2 windows containing the move holds three same-colour stones, fourth point empty | the canonical bad shape, taught to every beginner |
| `tigers-mouth` | `praise` | an empty point adjacent to the move where exactly one on-board orthogonal neighbour is empty and every other is the mover's colour | the good connection, worth naming when the player finds it |
| `dumpling` | `note` | the move is part of a solid 2×2 block of its own colour, all four stones in one chain | heavy, and the player usually cannot see it |

Three notes on those rules, each of which a first draft got wrong:

- **A tiger's mouth is not a 2×2 shape.** Three same-colour stones inside a 2×2 with the
  fourth point empty has exactly one reading, and it is the empty triangle. A mouth is
  defined on the *mouth point*: stones at (0,0), (2,0) and (1,1) surrounding an empty
  (1,0). Written as a shared 2×2 test, both detectors would fire on identical stones and
  the coach would criticise every good mouth and never praise one.

  The predicate must be exactly as stated in the table, and the obvious looser version
  ("three same-colour neighbours, or two plus the edge") is wrong three ways. An enemy
  stone in the fourth slot still leaves three same-colour neighbours, but that is the
  broken mouth a poke created, and praising it would be embarrassing. At a corner, two
  same-colour neighbours make an opponent play *illegal* — that is an eye, not a mouth —
  while the real corner mouth (one same-colour neighbour, one empty) goes undetected. And
  written as "three or more", a one-point eye reports as a mouth. "Exactly one on-board
  neighbour empty, all others mine" is one predicate that handles interior, edge and corner
  with no special case: an opponent stone played there would have exactly one liberty.
  Corner points are excluded from praise anyway — a one-stone corner mouth is technically
  a mouth and rhetorically silly.
- **A mouth whose own stones are in atari is not a mouth.** The predicate above is about
  the empty point and says nothing about liberties, which leaves a hole: if a guard's last
  liberty IS the mouth point, an enemy play there captures rather than dies, and the shape
  is the opposite of what it looks like. Praising it puts the coach at its loudest one move
  before the player is punished, the worst version of the false positive Open Question 5
  warns about. Every guard chain must have two liberties or more, which is the one place
  the detector reaches for `chainAt`.
- **The two shapes are not mutually exclusive, and the severity order is what saves us.**
  Black at (0,0), (1,0), (0,1), (2,1) with (1,1) empty is a textbook empty triangle in the
  lower-left 2×2 *and* a textbook mouth at (1,1). Both detectors fire, both correctly.
  `praise` outranking `note` is what makes the coach say the kind thing. That only works
  while `empty-triangle` is `note` — see the severity note below, which is therefore
  load-bearing, not a preference.
- **`dumpling` is structural, not a ratio, and must include the move.** A liberty-per-stone
  threshold does not work: a 2×2 block in the open centre has 8 liberties over 4 stones,
  ratio 2.0; a fifth stone gives roughly 9 over 5, ratio 1.8. Real heavy shape sits around
  1.2 to 1.6, and a ratio low enough to be distinctive (under 1.0) only catches groups two
  moves from death — which is the `low-liberty-group` detector dropped below for fighting
  the belt system, returning under a nicer name. The solid 2×2 block is exact,
  threshold-free, and is what players mean by a dango. The move must be one of the four
  stones: "the chain contains a block somewhere" would re-report a clump formed forty moves
  ago every time the player extends that chain, which is both untrue as a remark and
  O(chain length) instead of O(1) — any point is in at most four candidate blocks.

  Accepted cost: a solid 2×2 is sometimes forced and correct, in corner life and death, in
  capture races, and when filling under a peep is the right connection. The detector cannot
  tell those from a dango. It ships as a `note`, once per game, so the damage is bounded; if
  the ten games show it firing on forced connections, the first tightening is to require the
  joined chain to be five stones or more.
- **`empty-triangle` ships as `note`, not `warn`.** It is the most common of the three and
  the one Open Question 5 flags as most often legitimate — connecting against a peep makes
  an empty triangle and is usually correct. The highest-frequency, highest-false-positive
  shape is a poor candidate for the sternest voice.

  **If it is ever promoted to `warn`, the collision above flips back**: positions that are
  both a mouth and an empty triangle would be scolded instead of praised, which is the exact
  failure this whole block exists to prevent. Promotion requires re-checking that ordering,
  and CI pins it — one of the negative tests is a position that fires both detectors and
  asserts the mouth wins.

Explicitly **out** of the first set, with reasons:

- `atari-self` and `low-liberty-group` — these fight a settled design. `hintsForBelt`
  (`Game.jsx:153`) deliberately removes atari highlighting as the player improves, and the
  promotion ceremony says so at `:581`: "Atari hints come off from here: you read your own
  liberties now." A coach warning about atari reinstates the training wheel the belt just
  took away. If they ever return, they must be gated on `hintsForBelt`.
- `ponnuki` — needs `opts.captured` (available as `rec.lastCaptured`), so it is the
  cheapest extension once the first three land. First on the list to add.
- `hane-at-the-head` — needs the previous position to know the enemy chain was exactly two
  stones before the move. Real work, deferred.
- `bamboo-joint`, ladder, net, table shape, dog's head, first-line crawl, cut point, thin
  wall — later.

### Voice contract

`src/content/commentary.js`, data only:

- Lines keyed `[shapeId].default` with optional `[shapeId][personaId]` overrides. The test
  requires `default` for every shape and does **not** require every persona to cover every
  shape — otherwise the fallback is dead code and the experiment costs 7 × 3 authored line
  sets instead of 3 plus the overrides that are actually fun to write.
- The fallback key is `default`, not `house`. There is no persona with id `house`; `house`
  is the theme and typeface default and reusing it here would confuse two systems.
- **Exclamation marks: none in commentary lines**, enforced by test, even though the
  reactive persona chatter beside it uses them freely ("Got one! Sorry about that."). This
  is deliberate, not an inconsistency: the opponent is excitable, the coach is calm. If it
  reads as two voices in one pane during the ten games, the rule is what changes.
- Simple words, short sentences, playful and philosophical rather than instructional.
  Tetsu and Yuki say different things about the same empty triangle.
- Honesty: the bot names what it sees. It never claims to know the best move, and it never
  claims a group is dead.

### Pacing

A pure selector, so no rule lands in the view:

```js
chooseRemark(findings, { spoken, moveNumber, personaId }) -> { line, shapeId } | null
```

`spoken` is a map of shape id to `{ count, lastMove }`; the view holds only that map. The
count is not optional bookkeeping — a last-move-number alone cannot enforce "at most one
repeat", since a shape spoken at move 10 and again at move 45 clears a thirty-move gate
again at move 80 and gets a third remark. Rules, as named data beside the selector: at most
one remark per move; at least six moves between any two remarks; a shape may repeat at most
once, and only after thirty moves, with a different line; `warn` outranks `praise` outranks
`note`.

The repeat rule exists for arithmetic reasons. Three shapes spoken once each, gated on a
six-move gap and on captures, caps the coach at three remarks per game and realistically
yields two — so ten games would produce about twenty remarks across seven personas. That
is too quiet to answer "annoying": the experiment would measure delightful-or-invisible,
which is a different question. Allowing one repeat roughly doubles the sample.

### Integration

**The experiment writes to the chat pane only, not to the record.** `say()` appends to
ephemeral React state and is independent of `rec`, so it sidesteps three problems at once:

1. `botTurn(next)` captures `next` in a closure and later does `setRec(conclude(next, r))`
   from its own captured `r` (`Game.jsx:246-263`). Any `setRec(withMoveComment(...))`
   issued between the human's move and that timeout is silently discarded.
2. `withMoveComment` overwrites rather than appends (`record.js`), so it would clobber a
   comment imported from an SGF.
3. The record is persisted and exported as SGF `C[]`, so writing coaching into a rated
   game's record would make the exported file a permanent document of assistance.

Record-writing is deferred to the archive phase, where the comment is folded into `next`
**before** it is handed to `botTurn`, and where the rated question is already answered by
premise 4.

Integration points in `Game.jsx`: after the human's move in `onPlay`, beside the existing
`userCapture` line and before `botTurn(next)` (`:295-320`), and the `spoken` map reset
where the greeting is re-seeded on a rematch (`:397`). The bot's own moves are **not** commented on in v1
— the coach talks about your shapes, not its own. That is why `opts.color` and
`Finding.color` are always `"b"` in v1; they stay in the signature because the archive
phase reads both colours out of stored games.

Timing: commentary is skipped on any move that captured a stone, whether or not a capture
line was said — the human capture line is gated on two or more stones, so a one-stone
capture would otherwise still draw a coaching bubble on top of it. It is also skipped for
six moves after the delayed `reply` chatter fires. Note that a
"not while `thinking`" rule would be dead code: `onPlay` runs before `botTurn` calls
`setThinking(true)`, and that setter is async anyway, so the `thinking` value closed over
in the handler is always `false`.

### The off switch

A per-game toggle on the table, off by default, labeled as coaching and as making the game
unrated. It is the instrument that measures the experiment — without it, "delightful or
annoying" has no reading — and it is what premise 4 requires. Off-by-default per game
means the founder arms it each game for ten games; that is accepted, because a profile-level
setting would make it too easy to forget the game is unrated.

**Sticky, never reversible.** Once a game has been coached it stays unrated for its whole
life, and the toggle disables itself rather than un-marking the game. Any other rule lets a
player take advice for fifty moves and then switch coaching off to collect the rating.

**Persistence is a correctness requirement, not a nicety.** `saveGame` stores
`mode: { kind, personaId, rank, key }` (`src/store/gameStore.js:25`, read back at `:58`). A coaching game
resumed after a refresh comes back with no coaching flag, and `conclude` rates it — exactly
the dishonesty premise 4 exists to prevent. Add `coaching` to the stored `mode`, read
tolerantly with a default of `false` (the safe direction), so no `GAME_STORE_VERSION` bump
is needed. Store the `spoken` map alongside it, or the coach repeats itself after a refresh
and the ten-game measurement is contaminated by what reads as a bug.

The unrated plumbing is three places, all small:

1. `conclude`'s `else if (persona)` rating branch (`Game.jsx:201`) — skip `rateAgainst`.
2. The caption (`:544`) — the call already reads
   `rated: !!persona && !duel && !master`, so coaching joins an established pattern as
   `&& !coaching`. `captionText` already takes `rated` and already prints "unrated".
3. The result-card fine print (`:512`), which currently hardcodes "Rated against a house
   player."

## Open Questions

1. **Is in-game shape talk delightful or annoying?** The whole experiment exists to answer
   this. Ten games is the sample, the founder is the judge.
2. **Does the club want a coach at all, or a room?** Unresolved — "all the above" twice.
   The experiment answers the first half; only the club being online answers the second.
3. **Does the calm-coach / excitable-opponent split read as one bot or two?** Decided by
   taste during the ten games.
4. **Archive cap, eviction, and location.** How many games, what falls off first, and
   whether it lives in localStorage (a few megabytes) or in the Worker's Durable Objects.
   All three are open and none is blocking the experiment. This is a larger decision than
   "pick a number".
5. **Does the detector ever get it wrong?** An empty triangle is sometimes correct — a
   sacrifice, a shortage-of-liberties tesuji, a ko threat. Detection is easy; knowing when
   to stay quiet is the hard part, and a false positive costs more trust than a missed
   shape.
6. **Neo-Human rating.** Premise 5 says unrated. That needs saying out loud on the table
   before anyone plays one.
7. **Does the experiment's success convert into club value?** Premise 4 plus premise 1
   means the coach's home is bot games, and the club wants human-versus-human, where the
   coach never runs. A delightful coach and a thriving club may turn out to be two products
   sharing a codebase. That is a legitimate outcome, but it should be visible now rather
   than discovered in month three. Post-game commentary on an archived human game is the
   bridge, and it is the reason the archive matters.

## Success Criteria

**Experiment (the next thing built):**
- `src/engine/shape.js` detects all three shapes, with a test per shape built from real
  positions, plus negative cases. The three that matter most: a tiger's mouth must **not**
  report `empty-triangle`, an empty triangle must **not** report `tigers-mouth`, and a
  four-stone 2×2 block in the open centre must report `dumpling` when the move is one of
  its stones and must **not** report it when the move merely extends a chain that already
  contained the block. `npm test` green.
- `src/content/commentary.js` has a `default` line set for every shape, at least two
  persona overrides, and a test enforcing coverage and the no-exclamation-mark rule, in
  the pattern of `library.test.js`.
- `chooseRemark` is pure and tested for pacing: one per move, six-move gap, severity
  order. No shape ships with `warn` in v1, so the ordering test builds a synthetic `warn`
  finding rather than a real one — the tier is reserved, not dead.
- `chooseRemark` is tested for the repeat rule as well: a shape may return once more after
  thirty moves, with a different line, and never a third time.
- Commentary appears in the bot chat during a coaching game; the toggle is visible, marks
  the game unrated in the caption and the result card, is sticky once armed, and survives a
  page refresh through `gameStore` — a resumed coaching game must not be rated.
- `npm run build` and `npm run lint` pass.
- Ten games played by the founder, who then answers: delightful or annoying.

**Feature (later):** the club plays on Joseki; games are archived; the coach can say
something true about a shape you formed last week.

## Dependencies

- None for the experiment. It needs `chainAt` alone, already exported through
  `src/engine/index.js`, and only to check that a tiger's mouth's own stones are not in
  atari. (`chainsInAtari` and `withMoveComment` were dependencies of an
  earlier draft; the atari shapes were dropped and record-writing deferred, so neither is
  used now.)
- The archive depends on Open Question 4 being settled — cap, eviction, and localStorage
  versus Durable Objects. That is a design decision, not a line of code.
- Neo-Human depends on the multiplayer Worker's seat model and on Neo-Human games being
  explicitly unrated.

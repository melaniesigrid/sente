## Problem Statement

Joseki has rooms but no voices. `Room` holds a game and its chat, review stays
open after the last move, spectator sockets have worked since the first day, and
`Club` holds halls full of people. Every place where two people might want to
talk already exists as a Durable Object with both of them connected to it. What
is missing is audio.

The ask was voice during a game, voice during shared review, a direct call
between two players, voice in shared rooms, and a broadcast to everybody
watching a game. The last two are cut; see Premises and Scope of v1.

The interesting part is not getting audio between two browsers. It is doing it
without breaking the sentence in `src/content/legal.js` that says there is no
third company, and without claiming a security property the code does not have.

## What Makes This Cool

**The signalling server is treated as the adversary.**

Peer-to-peer WebRTC audio is already encrypted between the two browsers with
DTLS-SRTP. No relay can read it. That is not the gap. The gap is that the
Session Description Protocol offer carries the DTLS fingerprint, and the
signalling server is what relays that offer. A hostile or compromised `Room`
Durable Object can substitute its own fingerprints on both sides, terminate two
separate DTLS sessions, and sit in the middle of the whole game. Both players
see every indicator their browser has, and every indicator says encrypted.

Essentially everyone shipping peer-to-peer voice trusts their own signalling
server not to do this. This design does not.

The defence is ZRTP's Short Authentication String — the real one, commitment
included — rendered in the one vocabulary a go server already owns. Both players
run a committed key agreement in a Go module compiled to WebAssembly, the agreed
secret is bound to the DTLS fingerprints each side actually sees, and the result
is reduced to three go terms.

Black's screen says the terms. White's screen says the same terms. One of them
reads them aloud over the audio channel that was just opened, and the other hears
their own screen read back to them.

Underneath the terms, the same secret renders a position on a 5x5 board, drawn by
the `StoneFace` that draws every other stone in the app.

The portfolio sentence is: *the signalling server in this app is assumed hostile,
key agreement runs in Go compiled to WebAssembly, and two players confirm it by
saying a go term out loud to each other.*

**What that sentence does not claim**, and the design must never let it drift
into claiming:

- It authenticates a *channel*, not a *person*. Against a stranger, an attacker
  can simply be the stranger, and the verification will honestly confirm a
  channel to the attacker. The "fake their voice" difficulty only exists for an
  opponent you have heard before.
- It defends against a compromised Worker, not against a compromised operator.
  The defence works because GitHub Pages serves the app and the Cloudflare Worker
  only relays signalling, so a hostile Worker cannot also serve a lying client.
  One person holds both accounts. This is a real limit, it is stated here, and
  the privacy notice must not blur it.
- **The trust boundary is not the WASM module.** The Go module holds the hash,
  the ECDH and the refusal logic. Reading the certificate fingerprint, parsing
  and re-checking the SDP, holding commit-before-reveal on the wire, invalidating
  on renegotiation, and driving the card all live in JavaScript. "Key agreement
  runs in Go" is true. "The security lives in the WASM module" would not be.
- Cloudflare sees that two addresses exchanged encrypted media, and when. Forced
  relay buys privacy from your opponent, not from the relay.

## Constraints

From `CLAUDE.md` and `src/content/legal.js`, all of them load-bearing:

- **The privacy notice names two companies and says "Nobody else."** Any voice
  vendor is a third company and rewrites that clause. This design adds none.
- **`legal.test.js` holds the prose to what the code does**, with a revision
  stamp recomputed from the text. A claim in the notice that the code does not
  implement fails the suite. This is the reason revision 1 of this document was
  rewritten rather than shipped.
- **"Being here is not written down."** A call must not become a presence record.
- **Rules never live in a view, and the engine is pure.** Signalling is not a
  rule of go, so none of it belongs in `src/engine/` or in `server/room.js`.
- **Two-shadow neumorphism, Lucide icons only.**
- **Nine languages, one right-to-left.** Nine copies of `legal.js` under
  `src/i18n/`, roughly 270 lines each, plus a parity test.
- **`voice` is a taken namespace.** `src/i18n/*/voice.js` is the app's *prose*
  voice — clock phrasing, duel results, Moku's lines. The audio feature is called
  **talk** everywhere: `talk/` for the Go module, `talk/*` for the frames,
  `talk.wasm` for the artifact.
- **The beta is capped near a hundred players.** Concurrency is small.

## Premises

Agreed in session, all eight, with two amended after review:

1. **Spectator broadcast is out of v1.** Mesh cannot fan one speaker out to forty
   listeners. Viewers keep text chat.
2. **A shared room is a small room.** *Amended:* rooms above two are out of v1
   entirely, not merely capped. Pairwise verification is N(N-1)/2 rituals, which
   is fine at two and absurd at four, and open question 2 has no answer yet.
3. **No audio is ever stored.** Not recorded, not buffered, not pinned to a move.
4. **Signalling rides the existing Durable Objects.** *Amended:* `Room` only.
   `Club` is out of v1.
5. **The signalling server is treated as hostile.** Committed key agreement in
   Go/WASM bound to the DTLS fingerprint. No second encryption layer over the
   frames, because peer-to-peer DTLS-SRTP already covers them and a layer that
   protects against nothing is a sentence the privacy notice cannot carry.
6. **TURN is required.** *Amended, and this is now a privacy decision rather than
   a connectivity one:* every call is forced through TURN. See Addresses, below.
7. **The legal documents change in the same PR**, all nine translations, with
   `REVISION.stamp` and `UPDATED_ISO` moved.
8. **The mic is opt-in every session and push-to-talk by default.**

## Scope of v1

Ships: two people, both seated at the same `Room`, talking during a game or
during the review that follows it. Push-to-talk. Committed key agreement.
Verification card. Per-peer block. Nine legal translations.

Does not ship: halls and `Club` audio, rooms above two, spectator broadcast,
spectators talking at all, latched-open mic, recorded commentary, spoken
coordinates lighting the board, account-bound identity keys.

## Approaches Considered

Two decisions were taken in session. Each records the option that won and one
line on each that did not.

### Decision 1: where the media plane lives

**Chosen: peer-to-peer mesh, no media server.** Audio goes browser to browser.
The Durable Object relays signalling and never sees a media packet.

Ruled out: a *Go SFU on Fly or a VPS* (the strongest portfolio piece and the only
thing that delivers broadcast, but it costs a third host, the first always-on
bill, and the "Nobody else" clause); *Cloudflare Realtime SFU with frame
encryption* (broadcast with no new company, but the relay is somebody else's);
*everything through one Go server* (one code path, but a round trip two people
did not need).

### Decision 2: how the key agreement works

**Chosen: spoken verification in go vocabulary**, over a committed ephemeral
X25519 exchange, with the 5x5 stone position as a visual aid.

Ruled out: *stone position comparison with no key exchange* (sound and tiny, but
detection rather than prevention); *account-bound identity keys* (real prevention
with no human ritual, but lost keys, new devices, cleared storage and guests with
no account to bind to make it a larger project than the feature — kept as a v2
upgrade for signed-in pairs, never as a replacement, because guests need the
spoken path regardless).

## Recommended Approach

### The protocol

This is the part revision 1 got wrong, so it is specified move by move. **The
ordering is the security property.** It cannot be left to the implementer.

The initiator is whoever presses the mic first, and **each side asserts its
claimed role in its first frame.** Role assignment is mediated by the Durable
Object, so the adversary chooses it: it can tell both peers they are the
initiator, both that they are the responder, or reflect a commit back at its
sender. Matching claims abort immediately with a distinct "could not agree a
role" reason rather than hanging. Disagreement also fails closed on its own,
because the HKDF slots are fixed by role and a mismatch simply makes the two SAS
values diverge — but a reader should not have to derive that, so it is written
here. Role confusion is not a key-recovery attack; it is a silent, repeatable
denial of service, and it is handled together with the retry budget below.

**Getting the fingerprint before the first frame.** Generate the certificate with
`RTCPeerConnection.generateCertificate()`, construct the peer connection with
`{ certificates: [cert] }`, call `createOffer()`, and parse `a=fingerprint` out of
the returned local SDP. The fingerprint comes from the certificate, so it is
present and final; `createOffer()` transmits nothing and the offer sits in memory
until step 3 sends it. This uses only universally supported API and reuses the
same SDP parsing the step 3 and step 4 checks need anyway.
`RTCCertificate.getFingerprints()` is the tidier path where available, but it is
not the specified one — the protocol must not rest on a capability that might not
be there.

**Canonical fingerprint form**, because `go test` and vitest share vectors and
would otherwise disagree on day one: sha-256 only, algorithm token plus lowercase
colon-separated hex. An SDP may carry a session-level fingerprint and one per
m-line; **any SDP containing more than one distinct fingerprint value is
rejected**, rather than the first one found being checked. The step 3 and step 4
checks read `pc.remoteDescription.sdp` after `setRemoteDescription` — the
browser-normalised text it actually validated the certificate against — never the
raw relayed string.

```
  I (initiator)                                      R (responder)
  ─────────────                                      ─────────────
  cert_I → fp_I
  ephemeral X25519 → pub_I

  1.  talk/commit ──────────────────────────────────────────────►
      H( v ‖ roomID ‖ pub_I ‖ fp_I )
      Reveals nothing. R cannot choose against it.
                                                      cert_R → fp_R
                                                      ephemeral → pub_R
  2.  ◄────────────────────────────────────────────── talk/reveal
                                                      ( pub_R , fp_R )
      R is now bound. R chose without knowing pub_I or fp_I.

  3.  talk/reveal + SDP offer ──────────────────────────────────►
      ( pub_I , fp_I ) , offer(cert_I)
      R checks H matches step 1, and that the offer's
      fingerprint equals fp_I. Either check failing aborts.

  4.  ◄──────────────────────────────────── SDP answer(cert_R)
      I checks the answer's fingerprint equals fp_R.

  5.  Both compute, in fixed slots by role, never sorted:
      secret = X25519(own_priv, peer_pub)
      key    = HKDF(secret, info = v ‖ roomID ‖ fp_I ‖ fp_R
                                     ‖ pub_I ‖ pub_R ‖ wordlist_version)
      SAS    = 18 bits → three go terms
               40 bits → a 5x5 stone position
```

**Why the commitment is load-bearing.** Consider a hostile Room `M` relaying both
legs. On the leg where `M` plays initiator toward `R`, it must send its
commitment at step 1 before `R` reveals at step 2 — so it must fix its own key
and certificate without knowing `R`'s, and the SAS on that leg is determined
before `M` can influence it. On the leg where `M` plays responder toward `I`, it
must reveal at step 2 before `I` reveals at step 3 — so again it chooses blind.
`M` cannot make the two SAS values agree by search. It gets exactly one guess at
2^-18 per attempt.

**Without step 1** — which is what revision 1 specified — `M` knows every input
on both legs and freely chooses four of them: two ephemeral keys and two
self-signed certificates. Finding a collision in 16 bits by birthday search is
roughly 256 key generations per leg, which is milliseconds. Both players would
see *matching* terms, say them aloud, hear agreement, and be listened to, with
the ritual having reassured them. The commitment is the entire difference between
a security feature and a confidence trick.

**Retries are refused, and refusal alone is not enough.** The commitment gives one
blind guess per exchange, so a restart within the same room session is refused
rather than re-run. But the adversary controls the sockets: it can drop a
connection and force a reconnect at will, and a page reload clears in-memory
state, so a refusal flag cannot by itself hold the retry budget. The honest
position, and the one the interface is built on: each attempt is an independent
2^-18 guess, a failed guess shows the humans a mismatch, and **repeated
verification failures or role aborts with the same peer are surfaced as a
suspected attack, not as connection flakiness.** That framing is what actually
protects the budget. "The same room session" means one peer pair within one page
load; the scope is stated because it is the whole rule.

**Renegotiation invalidates verification.** An ICE restart or any renegotiation
that changes a fingerprint voids the SAS. Audio mutes and the card returns.

### Words and pictures

**Three terms, from a 64-entry list, is 18 bits.** Not two from 256. Sixty-four
is what a realistic audit of go vocabulary yields once confusable pairs are
struck out — *hane/hana*, *nobi/nozoki*, *sente/gote*, *tesuji/tewari* are four
collisions found without trying — and three short terms is no harder to say than
two. The list is Japanese go terminology, which is what players say in Munich,
São Paulo and Seoul alike, so the SAS needs no translation and does not enter the
i18n parity test as prose.

The **wordlist version is bound into the HKDF info string.** If the list is ever
edited, two clients on different deploys derive different terms from the same
secret, which would read exactly like an attack.

The **5x5 stone position renders 40 bits** from the same key. It is a visual aid
and an accessibility path, not a second channel: comparing it over the room's
text chat is worthless, because that chat is relayed by the Durable Object this
design declares hostile. The interface must say so — the picture is for comparing
in person, over a screen share, or over a call you already trust. Anything else
is decoration and will be labelled as such.

### Addresses

Peer-to-peer WebRTC hands your public IP address to the person you are talking
to. mDNS hides host candidates; the server-reflexive candidate is your real
address. On a product whose privacy notice lets you choose who may see that you
are *here*, handing a stranger your network location and coarse geography is a
regression, and it is not one the notice currently permits.

**So every call is forced through TURN:** `iceTransportPolicy: "relay"`. Peers
see a Cloudflare relay address and never each other's. Cloudflare is already a
named company in the notice and relays packets it cannot decrypt.

**The TURN host is compiled into the bundle, not handed out by the Worker.** This
is the part that is easy to get backwards. Forced relay guarantees that *all*
media traverses whichever host the ICE server list names — so if the Worker
supplies that list, a compromised Worker points every call in the product at a box
it controls. It still could not decrypt anything, because the SAS pins the DTLS
certificates, but it would learn both peers' real addresses on every call, which
is exactly the property this section exists to buy, and it could drop or shape
traffic invisibly. Forcing relay would hand the adversary the thing it was meant
to deny it.

So the TURN hostname is hardcoded in the client, which GitHub Pages serves and the
Worker does not. The Worker mints only short-lived credentials for that fixed
host, and the client refuses any ICE server whose host is not the compiled-in one.
The same Pages-versus-Worker separation that makes the whole threat model work
then also protects the address property.

This makes premise 6's "one connection in six" into six in six. At Opus rates
(24–32 kbps, roughly 15 MB per hour per direction) and a hundred beta players,
the bill is small. The privacy gain is not.

### The Go module

`talk/` at the repo root. Plain Go compiled with `GOOS=js GOARCH=wasm`, **not
TinyGo**: TinyGo would give roughly 100KB instead of 2MB, but its standard
library coverage of the crypto packages is the kind of thing that bites halfway
through, and the module is loaded lazily the first time somebody opens talk
rather than on every page view. Over Brotli on Pages it is 500–700KB, off the
critical path.

**Pin Go 1.24 or later in `actions/setup-go`.** `crypto/ecdh` is 1.20+, but
`crypto/hkdf` only entered the standard library in 1.24; below that the module
needs `golang.org/x/crypto/hkdf`, which reintroduces a dependency for no reason.
`wasm_exec.js` also moved from `misc/wasm/` to `lib/wasm/` in 1.24, so the copy
step must read the new path.

Exported across the WASM boundary:

- `newSession(role, roomID)` — ephemeral X25519 keypair, returns the public half.
- `commit(fingerprint)` — the step 1 hash. It takes only the fingerprint; the
  module already holds the key, and handing its own public half back to it is a
  mismatch waiting to happen.
- `receiveCommit(peerCommit)` — responder path only.
- `receiveReveal(peerPub, peerFingerprint)` — checks the reveal opens the
  commitment where one was received. Out-of-order calls and a second run are
  refused.
- `sas()` — three wordlist indices and the 5x5 position bits.

The module is a state machine with strict ordering, anti-replay, and a refusal
path, not thirty lines of arithmetic. That is what justifies writing it in a
language with `go test` rather than inline in the view.

**The shared vectors are mostly negative.** A state machine whose value is refusal
tested only on its happy path is untested. `go test` and vitest run the same
vectors for: out-of-order messages, a replayed commit, a reveal that does not open
the commitment, a second run in one session, matching role claims, and an SDP
carrying two distinct fingerprints. Each must refuse, with the same reason on both
runners.

### Signalling

New frame types on the existing socket: `talk/commit`, `talk/reveal`,
`talk/offer`, `talk/answer`, `talk/ice`, `talk/leave`.

**Relayed, not applied.** `server/room.js` is the pure reducer for the room
record and talk is not a rule, so none of these reach `applyMessage` and none are
persisted. `roomObject.js` forwards them to the addressed peer's socket and
forgets them. Nothing about a call is written to storage, which is what keeps
premise 3 and the "being here is not written down" clause true for free.

The relay is still a surface, so in the Durable Object: cap the payload size,
validate the frame shape even though it is not applied, rate-limit through the
existing `server/ratelimit.js`, and relay **only between two sockets that are
both seated and have both opted into talk**. Without that last rule the new path
is a covert channel between arbitrary clients.

Note for anything beyond v1: `roomObject.js:74` tags every non-seated socket with
the literal string `"spectator"`, and `emit()` resolves targets through
`room.seats`. There is no per-socket id for a watcher. Seated 1:1 works today;
spectators or halls need new tagging on the accept path first.

### The interface

- **The bowl lid is the mic.** Raised is muted, sunken is live. Two shadows in,
  two shadows out.
- **Push-to-talk**, hold the lid or a held key.
- **The call opens unverified, and says so.** Audio cannot be gated on confirming
  the SAS, because the SAS is confirmed *by speaking over the audio*. So the call
  starts in an explicit unverified state: a persistent banner, push-to-talk only,
  and the card on screen and undismissable until answered. The copy says plainly
  that until the terms are confirmed, what is said may be reaching the server.
  **The residual is real and belongs in the design rather than a footnote: an
  attacker who only wants the first twenty seconds wins.** That is ZRTP's own
  residual and it is the price of a human-mediated SAS. What must never return is
  a card that can be waved away and forgotten — the attacker's goal is to listen,
  not to be approved.
- **The card carries two controls**, *these match* and *these do not match*. The
  second tears the `RTCPeerConnection` down immediately and shows a persistent
  warning.
- **The initiator reads, the responder confirms.** Stated in the copy, so two
  people do not talk over each other in the one moment the design rests on.
- **The three terms render `dir="ltr"`**, including in Hebrew. Reversing their
  displayed order is a security bug, not a layout one.
- **Per-peer block**, surviving the session.
- **Mic permission denied, and the one-sided call** where only one player has a
  working mic, are designed states rather than dead ends.
- Spoken coordinates could light the board the way typed ones do via `parsePoint`
  (which lives in `src/engine/index.js`; `tableTalk.js` imports it). Out of scope.

### Conduct, and what cannot be investigated

The terms make harassment "grounds for removal the first time," and premise 3
means a voice report can never be substantiated. v1 narrows the exposure as far
as it can: talk is only ever with the opponent in a game you are both seated at,
never with a stranger in a hall, and per-peer block is in v1 rather than deferred.
The terms gain a clause saying plainly that nothing said over talk is recorded and
that reports about it cannot be investigated, only acted on by blocking.

## Open Questions

*(The `RTCCertificate.getFingerprints()` question that stood here was engineered
away rather than answered: the `createOffer` path in the protocol section needs
only universally supported API. A dependency you can remove beats a dependency
you can test.)*

1. **Sixty-four non-confusable go terms — does the list close?** The estimate
   above is an audit, not a count. If it lands nearer forty, use four terms
   instead of three to hold 18 bits, or qualify terms with a point on the 5x5
   board ("hane at C3") to buy width without losing the charm.
2. **Rooms above two.** Pairwise verification does not scale as a ritual. Either
   verify only against whoever invited you, or ship rooms unverified and say so
   in the interface — which would make the portfolio sentence false for that
   surface. Unresolved, and the reason rooms are out of v1.
3. **Cloudflare TURN quota and pricing at forced-relay volume.** Now every call,
   not one in six. The prose for the notice does not wait on this; the go/no-go
   does.
4. **Echo and the stone sounds.** `src/components/sound.js` synthesises the stone
   click through the speaker and the mic will hear it. Browser echo cancellation
   should handle the WebAudio output path; confirm rather than assume.
5. **Mobile Safari `getUserMedia` and the gesture requirement.** Same family as
   the AudioContext unlock footgun already in this project.
6. **Where "verified" is remembered** across a socket reconnect within one call,
   without writing anything to storage. **This is a prerequisite of the retry
   rule, not a loose end** — the adversary controls reconnects, so whatever
   answers it also decides how the repeated-failure warning is triggered.

## Success Criteria

- Two browsers on different networks hold a conversation during a game, with the
  Durable Object storing nothing about it.
- **The MitM test passes.** A simulated hostile Room substitutes *both* ephemeral
  public keys *and* both DTLS fingerprints and is allowed to search for a
  collision. The two SAS values diverge, and the attempt to restart the exchange
  is refused. **Write this test before any interface work** — it is the only test
  that can invalidate the design, and the single-field-substitution test from
  revision 1 would have passed green while the real attack succeeded.
- The same Go vectors pass in `go test` and in vitest, **negative vectors
  included** — out-of-order, replayed commit, non-opening reveal, second run,
  matching role claims, two-fingerprint SDP.
- The call is visibly unverified until the card is answered, the card cannot be
  dismissed unanswered, and *these do not match* tears the connection down.
- **Peers never learn each other's IP address, and it is tested:** the local
  description carries no `srflx` or `host` candidates and the selected candidate
  pair is `relay` at both ends.
- The client refuses an ICE server whose host is not the compiled-in one.
- `npm run build` passes; `legal.test.js` passes with new clauses and a moved
  stamp; the nine-language parity test passes.
- A player with no account can open talk, verify it, and be heard.
- Nothing in the privacy notice describes behaviour the code does not have.

## Session notes — not part of the design

Kept for the office-hours record; an implementer should stop at the line above.

- The request was "encrypted voice server," and *encrypted* came before any of
  the features. Most requests put it last, if at all.
- Go was raised unprompted, before the reason had been stated. The reason turned

  WebRTC" to "assume the signalling server is hostile" — which is the only part
  of this worth writing down.
- The full Go SFU was recommended and declined in favour of mesh. That is picking
  the version that ships over the version that impresses, in the same session
  where the stated goal was to impress.
- Revision 1 of this document specified a forgeable key agreement and an
  acceptance test that would have passed while the attack succeeded. It was
  caught by an adversarial review that had not seen the conversation. The lesson
  is narrow and worth keeping: "ZRTP-style" is not a design, and copying a
  protocol's output width while dropping its commitment step is the exact shape
  of a crypto mistake that reads fine in prose.

## What building it changed

Written after the five PRs, so the document and the code agree.

**"Talk" is the protocol; a "call" is what a person has.** The feature was
renamed once already, from `voice` (taken by `src/i18n/*/voice.js`, the app's
prose voice) to `talk` — and `talk` turned out to be taken too, by the chat:
`src/views/tableTalk.js`, and a top-level `talk:` key in `screens.js`. The
i18n index spreads its parts and the comment promises they never share a
top-level key, so adding one silently overwrote the chat's strings and six
etiquette tests went red. The same collision hit the stylesheet, where
`.talk-note` already belonged to the etiquette row.

So the layering is now explicit: `talk/` is the Go package, the wire frames and
the relay; `call.*`, `Call.jsx` and `useCall` are what a person opens. Three
namespaces were checked before the third name was used.

**Open question 1 is deleted, not answered.** The protocol no longer rests on
`RTCCertificate.getFingerprints()`. Both sides build a connection with their
certificate and call `createOffer()` purely to make the browser write the
fingerprint into an SDP, then throw it away. Universally supported, and the same
SDP parsing the later checks need anyway.

**Open question 2 is answered by measurement.** Sixty-four non-confusable go
terms do close; two hundred and fifty-six do not. `words_test.go` rejected
`atari`/`watari` and `sagari`/`magari` at one edit apart, and `nozoki`
swallowing `oki`. Three terms from sixty-four is the eighteen bits.

**The TURN host is `turn.cloudflare.com`.** A vanity name on our own domain
pointing at their service would have read like a third party the privacy notice
does not mention. `server/ice.js` imports the constant from `src/talk/ice.js`
rather than declaring its own, so the host credentials are minted for and the
host the browser accepts cannot drift.

**The nine translated legal documents were less work than budgeted.** Only seven
languages carry the documents at all; Hebrew shows English behind an honest
notice. The overlays are keyed by position, so the new section was inserted and
the keys renumbered — and the existing i18n guard, which already knew this trap
from the presence section, passed on it.

**Still not done, and honestly:** the browser has never run this. Every claim
above is checked by `go test`, by vitest, or by `tools/server/talk.mjs` against
a real Worker. None of that is two people on two machines hearing each other,
and open questions 3 through 6 — TURN pricing at forced-relay volume, echo
against the synthesised stone sounds, mobile Safari's gesture requirement, and
where "verified" lives across a reconnect — are all waiting on that.
